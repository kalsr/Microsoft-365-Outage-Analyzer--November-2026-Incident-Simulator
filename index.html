Microsoft-365-Outage-Analyzer--November-2026-Incident-Simulator

This is a Streamlit web app built by Randy Singh of Kalsnet (KNet) Consulting Group. It recreates the Microsoft 365 outage of 12–14 November 2026, when Exchange Online failures spread to Teams, SharePoint, Copilot, Defender and Power Platform. A region-by-region simulator steps through the outage in 5-minute intervals and follows the cause Microsoft described: a configuration change cut each server's capacity to set up secure (TLS) connections, slower servers failed their health checks and were removed, and the remaining servers took on their traffic and failed in turn. The simulator uses standard queueing math, where latency rises sharply as servers approach full load. From that model the app generates 1,000 to 1 million synthetic telemetry records, or it can analyse your real CSV/Excel monitoring data, and it automatically matches your column names to its own fields. Its pages cover the incident timeline, a "what-if" simulator with sliders for rollback timing, traffic throttling and health-check rules, TLS overload charts, impact by service and region (heat maps and a world map), the email backlog, alarm timing using a z-score, and SLA credits and cost. It is written in Python with pandas and NumPy for the data and math, Plotly for interactive charts, Matplotlib for report images, and ReportLab and python-docx for PDF and Word reports, with text and CSV exports too. In practice, it helps IT and operations teams understand why a small capacity loss turned into a day-long global outage. It tests whether fixes like earlier throttling, stricter health- check rules or faster rollback would have shortened it, measures how long it took to detect, and turns downtime into dollar figures for productivity loss and possible SLA credit claims. It also produces ready-made reports for executives and post-incident reviews, and teaches resilience lessons like staging configuration changes with automatic rollback. The server counts, user numbers and SLA credit tiers are made-up examples, and Microsoft's root cause was still unpublished, so the app models the cause Microsoft described, not a confirmed one.
